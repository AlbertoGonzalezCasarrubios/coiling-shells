Coiling Shells

Form and Colour

User manual

Alberto González Casarrubios

Contents

Coiling Shells builds the shells of snails, ammonites and clams from four numbers, adds ribs, cords and spines, and paints them with patterns that grow like those of real shells. This manual explains how to use it, step by step. The science behind it, the models of David Raup and Hans Meinhardt, is explained in the app itself, under About.

    1Getting started

    The app runs in the web browser, with nothing to install. Open albertogonzalezcasarrubios.github.io/coiling-shells in a recent version of Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari. It works best on a computer, and also on a tablet with touch gestures. The first time it needs an internet connection, to load the 3D engine and the formulas.

    On your first visit a welcome guide shows the parts of a coiled shell on a snail that you can turn with the mouse, and six short steps on how to use the app. Start exploring closes it and Read the theory opens the explanations. The Guide button opens it again at any time.

    Language and colours

    The EN and ES buttons, next to the title, switch the whole app between English and Spanish, and the app remembers your choice. It also follows the light or dark theme of your device; the Appearance menu changes the colours of the shell, the lines and the background (chapter 7).

    Nothing is lost. Every change is kept in the page address, and Ctrl Z (⌘ Z on a Mac) undoes it. Experiment freely.

    2The screen at a glance

    The sidebar on the left holds the controls; the 3D view of the shell fills the rest of the screen.

    1 Title of the app and the language buttons.
    2 Create your own shell: a short step-by-step window for building a shell (chapter 8).
    3 Challenge: a game in which you copy a hidden shell (chapter 9).
    4 The tabs Shape, Ornament and Pigment (chapters 4 to 6). The controls of the chosen tab are below them, and the Export… button at the very end (chapter 10).
    5 How the shell is drawn: Lines, Solid or Both. The arrow next to it undoes the last change.
    6 The toolbar: Equation, Axes, Appearance, Graphs, Guide, About, Fullscreen and Hide info.
    7 The standard views and the three ways of turning the shell (chapter 3).
    8 The 3D view. The shell is rebuilt as soon as you change a value.

    Many panels have a Theory → link next to their title, which opens the page of About that explains them.

    3Looking at the shell

    Mouse · touch What it does
    Drag · one finger Turn around the shell.
    Wheel · pinch Zoom in and out.
    Right-drag · two fingers Move the view.

    Standard views

    Malacologists, who study molluscs, draw shells from a few fixed directions. The bar at the bottom of the view gives each of them in one click: 3/4, a three-quarter view; Apertural, facing the opening with the apex up; Dorsal, from the opposite side; Apical, from the apex, along the coiling axis; Umbilical, from below; and Profile, from the side.

    The three buttons at the end of the bar choose how dragging turns the shell: around a vertical axis, the usual way for snails; around a horizontal one, for flat shells such as ammonites seen face-on; or freely, in any direction. After a free rotation, any standard view sets the shell upright again.

    Lines, solid or both

    Lines draws the shell as a mesh of spiral lines and growth lines, the way Raup drew his shells in the 1960s. Solid shows a surface with light and shadow; it is the mode that shows colour patterns. Both draws the lines on top of the surface.

    Only the shell

    Hide info, or the H key, hides every panel so that only the shell is left; H or Esc brings them back. Fullscreen, or F, fills the screen, and Axes shows or hides the small x, y, z axes in the corner.

    4Shape: Raup’s four numbers

    In 1966 the palaeontologist David Raup showed that a coiled shell is a tube that grows as it turns around an axis, and that four numbers are enough to describe it. The Shape tab has a slider for each.

    • W, whorl expansion rate. How much the tube widens in each turn. Low values give many tight whorls; high values a tube that widens very fast. A clam is a shell with a huge W.
    • D, distance from the axis. With 0 the whorls touch the axis; higher values open a hollow in the centre, the umbilicus, and the whorls draw apart.
    • T, translation rate. How far the tube moves along the axis as it grows. With 0 the shell is flat, like a nautilus; with high values it is tall and slender, like Turritella.
    • S, aperture shape. The shape of the opening: 1 is a circle, lower values flatten it and higher values make it taller.

    Drag a slider or type a value in its box; with a slider selected, the arrow keys move it step by step. W can be shown on a linear scale, from 1 to 10, or a logarithmic one, up to 104 to reach clams: choose with the Lin and Log buttons under its slider.

    Presets

    The Preset list, at the top of the tab, loads real shells: the garden snail, Turritella, a top shell, ramshorn and bladder snails, the nautilus, an ammonite and a clam, among others. It is the quickest way to start; change any value afterwards.

    Geometry

    • Number of whorls: how many turns are drawn, from the apex to the opening.
    • Coiling: Dextral, with the opening on the right as in most snails, or Sinistral, on the left.
    • Mirror valve: adds a mirrored copy, to make the two valves of a clam.
    • Animate growth: shows the shell growing from the apex to its final size.

    The morphospace

    Every combination of the four numbers is a possible shell. The Morphospace W – D panel, at the end of the Shape tab, is a map of those shells. The dot is your shell; the shaded regions are where real groups live: gastropods, ammonoids and nautiloids, and bivalves.

    Click or drag on the map to move to another shell, or click the name of a group to load a typical one. The buttons at the top change the range of W, so that clams fit on the map.

    Large parts of the map are empty: nature uses only a few of the possible shapes. Asking why was one of the reasons Raup drew this map. Try a point in an empty region and look at the shell you get.

    5Ornament: ribs, cords and spines

    Real shells are rarely smooth. The Ornament tab adds relief to any shape. The Ornamented shells list loads real examples with both their shape and their relief: a wentletrap with thin ribs, a top shell with a keel, a sun shell with spines, two ammonites, a cockle and others.

    • Axial ribs: ridges across the whorls, like the growth lines. Set how many per whorl, their height and width.
    • Spiral cords: ridges along the whorls. In clams they are the radial ribs that fan out from the hinge.
    • Nodules: bumps where ribs and cords cross.
    • Spines: points at regular intervals, in one row or in several (Rows and Gap between rows), centred on their Position (0 = the periphery, the middle of a bivalve valve): a band of spines, or a whole spiny valve as in the thorny oyster (Spondylus).

    The Simple / Advanced switch shows more controls: the asymmetry of the ribs, the position of cords and spines, a keel (a single sharp ridge), and Change with growth, which makes the relief fade as the shell grows, as in many shells that are smooth when adult, or appear only late. Remove ornament makes the shell smooth again.

    Why this number of cords?

    The advanced controls end with a small model that explains where cords come from. The shell is built by the edge of the mantle, the soft tissue that lines it. If that edge grows a little faster than the shell, it wrinkles into waves, like a rug pushed against a wall, and each wave leaves a cord.

    Two sliders set the balance:

    • Attachment / bending Λ: how firmly the edge is held to the shell, against how stiff it is. A stiff edge prefers few, wide waves; a firmly held one, many narrow ones.
    • Push of growth η: how much faster the edge grows. Below 1 it does not wrinkle and the shell stays smooth.

    The curve shows the push needed for each number of waves, and its lowest point, the dot, is the number that appears. The ring next to it shows the wrinkled edge. Grow these cords on the shell applies that number to your shell.

    6Pigment: patterns that grow

    A mollusc can only add colour at the edge of its shell, while it grows, so a shell pattern is a record of what happened along that edge over time. The physicist and biologist Hans Meinhardt simulated it with substances that react with each other and spread between neighbouring cells. The Pigment tab runs his simulations on your shell.

    Choosing a pattern

    When you open the tab the shell switches to Solid but keeps its plain colour: choose a pattern in the gallery to paint it. Each of the five comes from a real shell: the flames of the nautilus, the spiral bands of the banded snail Cepaea, the rows of spots of moon snails (Naticarius), the triangles of Oliva and the white tents of the marbled cone (Conus). Solid colour or Remove colour take you back to a plain shell. Patterns are computed, not drawn, so a new one can take a second or two; “Computing…” is shown meanwhile.

    Simple controls

    • Pattern size: how large each element is. It sets the number of cells along the edge.
    • Growth time: how long the shell takes to grow. More time leaves more stripes along the direction of growth.
    • Irregularity: from regular, tidy patterns to irregular, branching ones.
    • Pigment amount: makes the dark areas wider or narrower, without changing the simulation.
    • Seed and New seed: each seed is a different individual of the same species, as no two real shells are identical.
    • Pigment colours: the colours of the background and of the pigment.

    The space–time record

    Below the gallery, the Space–time record lays the whole pattern flat. The edge of the shell runs across and time runs down, from the apex to the opening. In time gives every whorl the same height, as in Meinhardt’s diagrams; scroll it up to see the first whorls. Unrolled shell draws it with the proportions of the shell, as a fan. Save pattern downloads it as an image.

    Advanced controls

    Advanced shows every constant of the equations, for those who want to explore the models themselves.

    • Model: activator–inhibitor or activator–substrate, the two kinds of reaction Meinhardt used. Changing it loads values that work.
    • Constants: how fast each substance spreads, decays and is produced, and the saturation.
    • Global control: a hormone that counts the waves, used by Oliva and Conus.
    • Gradient along the margin: makes the outer side of the whorl run faster, as in the nautilus, which has more stripes outside than inside.
    • Growing margin: adds cells as the shell grows, so that elements keep their size and new ones appear between the old.

    The margin profile plots the activator and its antagonist along the edge at one moment; click or drag on the record to choose the moment. Pigment is laid down where the activator rises above the dotted threshold.

    Near some values the pattern jumps from one type to another: that is how the models behave, not an error. If a combination forms no pattern at all, the app says so and suggests recipes.

    Graphs over the shell

    The Graphs menu places the morphospace and the margin profile over the 3D view, to watch them while you move the sliders. The slider under the profile moves along the growth of the shell.

    7Equations, theory and appearance

    Equation

    The Equation button opens a panel with the formula of Raup’s model, in general form and with the current values, which change as you move the sliders. With a pattern applied, a second tab shows Meinhardt’s equations.

    About

    About explains the science behind the app in five pages: Raup’s model of the shape, Meinhardt’s models of the pigment, the mechanics of the ornament, a summary of the controls, and the credits, with how to cite the app.

    Appearance

    The Appearance menu, in the toolbar, changes how the shell is drawn:

    • Colour presets, light and dark, and the colours of the lines, the shell and the background, each with its own colour picker.
    • Mesh resolution: low is faster on modest computers; high is smoother, for images.
    • The number of spiral lines and growth lines, and the width and opacity of the lines.
    • Hide back lines: in line mode, hides the lines behind the shell. Turned off, the shell looks transparent, like a wire sculpture, as on the cover of this manual.
    • Growth bands: faint bands on the solid surface that mark its growth.
    • Show coiling axis, and Auto-rotate to turn the shell slowly on its own.

    8Create your own shell

    Create your own shell is the easiest way to make a shell of your own. It opens a window with five steps, each with a few simple sliders, and the shell turning on the left:

    1. Shape: it starts from the garden snail; choose another real shell, or press Use the shell of the main view to bring in the one you were looking at. Then change how the tube grows.
    2. Ornament: add ribs, cords or spines, or load an example.
    3. Colour: choose a kind of pattern and its colours; Another variant of this pattern gives a new individual.
    4. Look: how the shell is drawn and on what background.
    5. Export: give it a name and take it with you as a wallpaper for your screen, as a PDF sheet, or in other formats.

    When you finish, the shell stays on the main screen, where every control can keep changing it. If you close the window without changing anything, the shell you had before comes back.

    9The challenge

    Challenge: match the shell is a game for learning what each number does. A target shell is shown above and yours below, turning together. Move the sliders until they look alike, then press Check.

    • There are eight levels in three blocks. Shape frees the parameters one at a time: W, then T, then D, then all of them. Ornament asks for the relief, and Colour for the pattern and its colours; the last level asks for everything at once.
    • The likeness compares the two shells seen from several sides. With 85 % in the first levels, and 90 % in the later ones, you pass the level; after each try a hint tells you what to change.
    • Another shell gives a new target in the same level. The app remembers how far you got.
    • Start again from level 1 forgets your progress, and the round arrow in the corner of each block plays that block again from its first level.
    • Closing the challenge gives you back the shell you had before.

    10Saving and sharing

    Export…, at the end of the sidebar, opens a window with every way of saving your shell. The optional name at the top becomes the title of the PDF and the name of the files.

    Option What you get
    Image of the current view Exactly what the 3D view shows, without panels, as PNG, JPG or TIFF: from the size of the screen to four times larger for printing, or at fixed sizes for wallpapers.
    Plate of standard views The six standard views on one image, without perspective, as in a scientific figure, with or without labels.
    PDF sheet An A4 report with the plate, the values, the morphospace and a link that reopens the shell. With a pattern it adds a page with the record, the margin profile and the constants, and optionally one with the equations.
    3D printing STL or 3MF files with a real wall thickness, in millimetres, ready for a slicer. A warning appears if the spines are too thin to print.
    3D model with colour A GLB file with the surface and its pattern, for 3D viewers, Blender or augmented reality.
    Pattern The space–time record of the pigment, as an image.
    Share a link. The page address always holds the current shell, with its ornament, pattern and appearance. Copy it from the browser, and anyone who opens it sees exactly the same shell.

    11Quick reference and tips

    Key What it does
    Ctrl Z · ⌘ Z Undo the last change.
    F Full screen.
    H Hide or show the panels.
    Esc Show the panels again, or close a window.
    Arrow keys Move the selected slider step by step.

    Ideas to try

    • Start from the garden snail and lower T to 0: the shell flattens like a ramshorn snail. Raise D and the whorls come apart.
    • Load the clam and look at W on the logarithmic scale: snails and clams are ends of a single slider.
    • Put the same pattern on a snail and on a nautilus: the pattern is the same, but the shape changes how it looks.
    • With the Cepaea pattern, press New seed several times: each seed is a different snail.
    • Draw a Turritella with lines on a black background and turn off Hide back lines.

    If something does not work

    • The shell does not appear. Use a recent browser with hardware acceleration (WebGL) turned on, and an internet connection the first time.
    • The formulas do not show. They are loaded from the internet; check the connection.
    • The pattern is not visible. Choose Solid or Both: in Lines there is no surface to paint.
    • “No pattern”. The values form no pattern; pick one from the gallery or follow the recipes shown.
    • Everything is slow. Lower the mesh resolution in Appearance, or the pattern size and the growth time.

    12Glossary

    Activator
    In Meinhardt’s models, the substance that switches pigment on and enhances its own production.
    Antagonist
    The substance that stops the activator: an inhibitor it produces, or a substrate it uses up.
    Aperture
    The opening of the shell, where the animal comes out and where the shell grows.
    Apex
    The tip of the shell: its oldest part, where growth began.
    Body whorl
    The last and largest whorl, which ends at the aperture.
    Coiling axis
    The imaginary line around which the tube turns.
    Cord
    A ridge that runs along the whorls, in the direction of coiling.
    Dextral, sinistral
    Coiled to the right or to the left: with the apex up and the aperture facing you, the aperture is on the right or on the left.
    Keel
    A single sharp ridge along the whorls.
    Mantle
    The soft tissue that lines the shell and builds it at its edge.
    Morphospace
    The space of all possible shapes; here, the shells given by W, D, T and S.
    Rib
    A ridge across the whorls, parallel to the growth lines.
    Space–time record
    The pattern laid flat: the edge of the shell across, and time, from the apex to the aperture, down.
    Spire
    All the whorls above the body whorl.
    Suture
    The line where one whorl meets the next.
    Umbilicus
    The hollow around the coiling axis, at the base of the shell, when the whorls do not touch it.
    Whorl
    One complete turn of the tube.
